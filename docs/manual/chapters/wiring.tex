\chapter{Wiring}
\label{ch:wiring}

Power in Immersive Engineering travels along wires strung between connectors. The wires sag, they
have length limits, they lose energy over distance, and at high voltage they will kill you. That is
the whole character of the mod in one system.

\section{The three tiers}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrr@{}}
\toprule
Tier & Wire & Max transfer & Loss & Max length \\
\midrule
LV & Copper    & 2\,048 IF/t  & 5\,\%   & 16 blocks \\
MV & Electrum  & 8\,192 IF/t  & 2.5\,\% & 16 blocks \\
HV & Steel     & 32\,768 IF/t & 2.5\,\% & 32 blocks \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Loss is proportional to how much of the wire's maximum length the run actually uses, so a short HV
run loses very little and a run at the limit loses the full percentage.

\begin{ietip}
Insulated copper and insulated electrum carry the same power as their bare versions and do not shock
anything that touches them. They cost more. On a build people walk through, pay it.
\end{ietip}

\section{Connectors and relays}

A \textbf{connector} is where a wire meets a machine: it takes power from the block it is attached
to, or gives power to it. A \textbf{relay} only passes wire to wire --- it is a pole, not a socket
--- and relays are how you get a line across a distance without a machine at every corner.

\begin{ietip}
A connector accepts only its own tier's wire. The wrong coil is simply refused --- nothing is
damaged --- but the refusal is announced only as a line in the status bar, and the coil clears its
first click exactly as it does on success. If a link seems not to have taken, read the chat.
\end{ietip}

\section{Transformers}

Step between tiers. The \textbf{Transformer} joins a low-voltage side to a medium-voltage one. The
\textbf{HV Transformer} takes high voltage on its high side and steps it down to \emph{either} MV or
LV --- going from HV to LV needs one transformer, not two.

A transformer is three blocks tall, and wire attaches only to the \emph{top} block. That block
carries two terminals on opposite faces, one per tier; aim at the correct half of it, because the
two blocks beneath it accept nothing. Mounted on a post, it takes its wires at the post instead.

\begin{ietip}
Neither transformer is symmetrical, and both refuse a wire silently. If a terminal will not take a
coil, you are either on the wrong face or on the wrong block of the three.
\end{ietip}

\section{Wire coils and the wirecutter}

Wire is placed by holding a \textbf{coil} and right-clicking one connector, then the other. The
\textbf{Engineer's Wirecutter} removes a wire and returns the coil.

\section{Posts and wallmounts}

\textbf{Wooden}, \textbf{Steel} and \textbf{Aluminium Posts} are what you actually string wire
between across country. A post carries arms, which give you somewhere to hang connectors, relays
and transformers off the pole itself rather than needing a building behind them.

\textbf{Wooden}, \textbf{Steel} and \textbf{Aluminium Wallmounts} are the same idea against a wall:
a bracket that stands a connector or a transformer off the face of a building, so a line can reach
it without the hardware sitting flat against the brick.

\section{Breaker switches and meters}

\begin{description}
\item[Breaker Switch] A manual on/off in a wire run. Right-click to throw it. It joins at most two
      wires of any one tier, and it \emph{emits} a redstone signal while closed rather than
      listening for one; a hammer inverts that.
\item[Redstone Breaker] The same cut-off, driven by an incoming redstone signal instead of by hand.
\item[Current Transformer] The meter. Two blocks tall and spliced into a run, it passes power
      through like a relay while measuring it. Right-click it to read the average throughput and
      packet count. It also drives a \textbf{comparator} from 0 to 15, scaled against the transfer
      rate of the wire it sits on --- so a saturated line reads 15 and a half-loaded one reads 8.
\end{description}

\section{Feedthroughs}

An insulator that carries a wire through a wall without breaking the run, so a line can enter a
building without a connector on each side.

\section{Wire damage}

An energised HV wire damages anything that touches it, and an overloaded wire burns out and drops
as scrap.

\begin{iefork}
City mode changes both. Wire burnout is disabled outright, and shock damage becomes local: rather
than every powered connector broadcasting to the whole network every tick on the chance somebody is
touching a wire, a node that needs the information asks for it. That single broadcast was the most
expensive thing the mod did on a profiled server. \autoref{ch:citymode}.
\end{iefork}

\begin{iefork}
This fork also offers two alternatives to wire entirely: the virtual grid (\autoref{ch:grid}) for
distance, and conduits (\autoref{ch:conduits}) for indoors.
\end{iefork}
